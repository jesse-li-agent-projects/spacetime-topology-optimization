\section{Introduction}
% Short description of the assignment, its relevance and the field. (1 page)
Additive manufacturing (AM), where material is incrementally deposited, and topology optimization, where a computer algorithm automatically designs a part's toppology and geometry in response to design objectives such as compliance, are two synergistic techniques. Topology optimization often results in complex part geometry, which AM techniques generally excel at manufacturing.

Traditionally, 3-axis AM deposits material in uniform planar layers. However, the restriction in fabrication sequence introduces design constraints, depending on the specific AM technology being used. For example, fused deposition modelling limits the maximum overhang a part can have without separate support material. As another example, wire arc additive manufacturing results in substantial heat load applied to the in-progress part, which risks overheating if there is insufficient nearby material to conduct away heat.

Multi-axis AM offers more flexibility to tackle these challenges, by allowing curved layers and customizable fabrication sequences. With this increased flexibility comes the new design challenge and opportunity of choosing the fabrication sequence, to improve performance.
Moreover, by using a topology optimization formulation that is aware of the fabrication sequence, the resulting design can potentially perform better than sequential optimization of part topology and fabrication sequence.

Wang et al. \cite{wang2020spacetime} introduce space-time topology optimization (STTO), which simultaneously optimizes a density field (defining the part geometry) and a time field (defining the fabrication sequence), and use it to minimize compliance of intermediate structures as well as the final structure.
\cite{das2023thesis} introduces a physically-motivated geometric method to efficiently estimate overheating severity, including a simplified approach suitable for STTO, and designs parts subject to a maximum overheating constraint.
% Note: Manav's approach uses direct time formulation
\todo{Should I be citing Manav's thesis, which already has an early STTO formulation? Or Manav's paper, which just focuses on TO with planar fabrication?}

A key requirement for physically realizable time fields is that they have no interior local minima or maxima.
A local minimum in the time field corresponds to unsupported material, while a local maximum corresponds to fully surrounded material that cannot be physically accessed for material deposition.
\cite{wang2020spacetime} and \cite{das2023thesis} address this through an explicit continuity constraint which ensures each internal element has neighours which are larger and smaller than it.
\cite{Wang2024Regularization} represent time as the solution to a heat equation PDE with a sink term, to avoid local minima by construction; however this formulation still permits local maxima.
\cite{Xu2025CurvedLayerSlicing} also represent time as the solution to a heat equation PDE, but use manually-specified non-homogenous Dirichlet boundary conditions rather than a sink term.
They additionally use a second step of fitting a field to the normalized temperature gradient by solving a Poisson equation, to obtain near-uniform layer thickness.

This work extends \cite{das2023thesis} and develops a more flexible and robust STTO approach. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Refinements to the optimization objective, which improve realism near edges, account for printing direction effects on overheating, promotes layer uniformity, and improves optimization dynamics
    \item A formulation for the time field as the solution to a steady-state diffusion equation with optimizer-chosen boundary conditions, used directly as the time field rather than as an input to a Poisson fit as in \cite{Xu2025CurvedLayerSlicing}, which automatically prevents local minima and maxima in the time field (a common challenge in STTO)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Topology formulation}
We use a standard density-based formulation of topology optimization, utilizing solid isotropic material with penalization (SIMP) \cite{Bendsoe1989SimpFoundation} \cite{Zhou1991SimpFoundation}, density filtering \cite{BRUNS20013443DensityFilter}, and projection \cite{Guest2004DensityProjection, Wang2011RobustProjection}, and consider a linear elastic compliance minimization problem.

Beginning with the density design variable $\rho_d$, defined for each element $i$ as $\rho_{d,i}$, we apply filtering to get filtered density $\rho_f$, to avoid checkerboarding and introduce a minimum length scale:
\[\rho_{f,i} =  \frac{\sum_j w (\mathbf{x_i} - \mathbf{x_j}) \rho_{d,j}}{\sum_j w (\mathbf{x_i} - \mathbf{x_j})} \qquad w(\mathbf{x_i} - \mathbf{x_j}) = \max \left(0, 1-\frac{\norm{\mathbf{x_i} - \mathbf{x_j}}}{r_{f}}\right)\]
We apply hyperbolic tangent projection \cite{Wang2011RobustProjection} to promote binarization and get physical density $\rho$:
\[\rho_i = \frac{\tanh(\beta \nu) + \tanh\left(\beta (\rho_{f,i} - \nu)\right)}{\tanh(\beta \nu) + \tanh\left(\beta (1 - \nu)\right)}\]
$r_f$ is the filter radius, $\beta$ is the projection sharpness, and $\nu$ is the projection threshold.

Each element has an elastic modulus $E_i$, interpolated with SIMP, with
\[E_i(\rho_i) =  E_{min} + \rho_i^p (E_0 - E_{min})\]
where $E_0 = 1$ is the elastic modulus of a solid element and $E_{min} = 10^{-9}$ is a small non-zero value to avoid numerical singularities in void elements.

We consider the compliance $c$, given by
\[c(\rho) = \mathbf{U}^T \mathbf{KU} = \sum^{N_e}_{i=1} E_i(\rho_i) \mathbf{u}_i^T \mathbf{k}_0 \mathbf{u}_i \qquad \mathbf{KU} = \mathbf{f}\]
where $N_e$ is the number of elements, $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are the global displacement vector and stiffness matrix respectively, $\mathbf{u}_i$ is the element displacement vector for element $i$, $\mathbf{k_0}$ is the element stiffness matrix for an element with unit elastic modulus, and $\mathbf{f}$ is the global nodal force vector.

\subsection{Time field formulation}
Let $\Omega$ denote the design domain over which to optimize, and $\partial \Omega$ be the domain boundary. Let $\partial \Omega_p$ be the boundary corresponding to the build plate. Let $\mathbf{x}$ denote the spatial coordinates.
We define the raw time field $t_r$ as the solution to the steady state diffusion equation

\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot\big(\chi\nabla t_r\big) = 0, \quad t_r(\mathbf{x}) = 0 \ \forall \mathbf{x} \in \partial \Omega_p, \quad t_r(\mathbf{x}) = b(\mathbf{x}) \ \forall \mathbf{x} \in \partial \Omega \backslash \partial \Omega_p
    \label{eqn:time_field_laplace}
\end{equation}

where $b(\mathbf{x})$ is a nonhomogenous Dirichlet boundary condition, $\chi = \max\left(\rho^a, 10^{-5}\right)C^{\mu(\mathbf{x}) - 0.5}$ is a non-uniform diffusivity coefficient, $\rho$ is the density, and $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ is a density-uncorrected diffusivity coefficient.

Numerically, $b$ is defined at the boundary mesh nodes and linearly interpolated within an element.
The boundary condition $b$ is meant to be optimized, but we find smoother results are achived by having $b$ be a filtered version of a raw boundary condition design variable $b_r$:
\[b(s_k) = \frac{\sum_j w_b(s_k - s_j) \tilde{b}_r(s_j)}{\sum_j w_b(s_k - s_j)} \qquad w_b(d) = \max\left(0, 1 - \frac{\abs{d}}{r_b}\right)\]
where $s$ is the arc length along the boundary $\partial \Omega \backslash \partial \Omega_p$, $s_j$ are the positions of the nodes, and $r_b$ is the wall filter radius.

Near the build plate corners where the designed boundary condition ends, the filter would average over nodes on one side only, and $b$ would suddenly jump above zero right next to the build plate, where $t_r = 0$.
To avoid this discontinuity, we instead filter $\tilde{b}_r$, which virtually extends $b_r$ past each end by reflecting it with its sign flipped, $\tilde{b}_r(-s) = \tilde{b}_r(2L - s) = -b_r(s)$, where $L$ is the length of the designed boundary condition with $\tilde{b}_r = 0$ at the ends themselves; the sums in the filter run over the nodes of this extended wall.
This ensures $b$ goes to zero where the wall meets the build plate.

We consider $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ and $b_r(\mathbf{x})$ to be design variables to optimize for.
While one can directly optimize over $\chi$ instead of $\mu$, the optimization problem is better behaved if we optimize over $\mu$; this formulation allows $\chi$ to extend over several orders of magnitude and automatically change with $\rho$ while $0 \leq \mu \leq 1$ stays order 1.
$a$ and $C$ are constant hyperparameters; we find $a=4$ and $C=1000$ to work well. $b_r$ is constrained to satisfy $0 \leq b_r \leq 1$.

A key property of this formulation is that in the interior of $\Omega$, there are no local minima or maxima.
Moreover, \autoref{sec:time_field_generality} shows that any sufficiently smooth time field $t_r$ that is zero on the build plate and has no critical points (and thus strictly positive layer thickness everywhere) is the solution to \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} for some choice of $\chi$ and $b$. Thus, this formulation does not restrict the design space of valid time fields.

This formulation does not rule out the existence of local minima on the design space boundary, which are still problematic for physical reasons. However, in practice this rarely occurs, because a local minimum in time also tends to have high hotspot severity, as defined in \autoref{sec:hotspot_formulation}, because it has no supporting material.

We scale the time variable to be from 0 to 1 across the part, by dividing by the maximum time in material that would binarize to solid. Specifically, we compute $t_{max}$ as the largest time over elements where $\rho_i > \max \rho / 2$. We compute the binarization threshold relative to the maximum density rather than using 0.5, because initializing the design space uniformly in density with volume fraction less than 0.5 would then result in no elements satisfying the threshold. We then compute the (actual) time field with $t = t_r / t_{max}$.

The wall design variable $b_r$ is initialized to the geodesic distance across the design domain from the build plate\footnote{For implementation simplicity, the geodesic distance is approximated by building a discrete graph with 8-connected edges and using shortest-path algorithms on the graph.}, divided by $2 d_{max}$, where $d_{max}$ is the largest such distance on the wall. $t$ is invariant to scaling $b_r$ because of the rescaling to ensure $0 \leq t \leq 1$ over the part; the normalization constant $2 d_{max}$ is thus only to ensure $b_r$ is of order 1 and unconstrained above on initialization.  Since $t$ does not change when $b$ is scaled, this scaling does not restrict the time field; it only places $b_r$ in the middle of its bounds for the optimizer. $\mu$ is initialized to a uniform constant (0.5).

\subsection{Hotspot severity formulation}
\label{sec:hotspot_formulation}
To assess the risk of overheating at any given material point, we define a metric $K$ on each element to estimate the heat evacuation capacity\footnote{\cite{das2023thesis} refers to this as \emph{Conductivity Estimation}; to avoid ambiguity with conductivity as a material property we use the term \emph{heat evacuation capacity estimate}.}; a higher $K$ corresponds to a better ability to evacuate heat and reduced risk of overheating. Since $K$ is computed on a per-element basis and requires element print time as an input, we use the mean print time for that element, which for Q4 bilinear elements, is equal to the time at the geometric center and the mean of the time at the four nodes.

We compute $K_e$ for each element $e$ with:
\[
    K_e = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{j \in V_e} w_{ej}\,\lambda_{ej}\, F(t_e - t_j)s(\rho_e, \rho_j),
    \qquad
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_e$ is the set of elements in a radius $r_K$ circle centered at element $e$
    \item $w_{ej} = (r_K - d_{ej})/r_K$ is a radial weight, where $d_{ej}$ is the Euclidean distance between element $j$ and element $e$
    \item $\lambda_{ej} = \exp\left(-\kappa (\nabla t \cdot \hat d_{ej} + |\nabla t|)/(|\nabla t| + g_0)\right)$ is an angular weight, favouring neighbours behind the print front, with $\hat d_{ej}$ being a unit vector in the direction from element $e$ to element $j$.

          $\kappa$ and $g_0$ are hyperparameters, with the first controlling the anisotropy of the angular weight and the second promoting an isotropic weight when $\nabla t$ is small and therefore the printing direction is uncertain. \autoref{fig:angular_weight} shows the effect of $\kappa$ on $\lambda$.
    \item $F$ is a term to ensure only material printed earlier in time contributes to $K$:
          \[
              F(\Delta t) = 1 - \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{softplus}\big(k\,\Delta t/\tau\big)\Big), \qquad \tau = 0.01, \qquad \mathrm{softplus}(x) \defeq \ln \left(1 + e^x\right) .
          \]
          $k$ and $\tau$ are hyperparameters. $k$ controls the sharpness of $F$, with its effect visualized in \autoref{fig:cooling_credit}. $\tau$ controls the time scale used in $F$ and can be tuned so that the material printed in $\delta t = \tau$ is one layer thickness of material.

          At $k=1$, $F$ reduces to the sigmoid function $\sigma(t/\tau) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-t/\tau}}$, often used in prior work as a soft mask for material printed before a given time. However, the sigmoid function has the property that $F(0;\  k=1) = \sigma(0) = 0.5$, which means that a constant (trivial) time field can easily achieve a high $K$.

    \item $s(\rho_e, \rho_j)$ is a term ensuring lower density material contributes less to $K$:
          \[
              s(\rho_e, \rho_j) = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\rho_j + \varepsilon_s}{\rho_e + \varepsilon_s}\right)^{q}, \qquad \varepsilon_s = 10^{-3},
          \]
          $q$ is a constant hyperparameter.

    \item $D(\kappa)$ is a normalization constant in space, chosen so that an ideal uniform planar-layered print has $K_e = 1$. $D$ can be computed by considering an infinite domain with uniformly increasing time field $t=x$, and computing
          \[
              D = \sum_{j \in V_e} w_{ej}\lambda_{ej} H(t_e-t_j)\qquad H(\Delta t) = \begin{cases}
                  1   & x > 0 \\
                  0.5 & x = 0 \\
                  0   & x < 0 \\
              \end{cases}
          \]
          for any element $e$, with $g_0 = 0$. Note that this normalization means $K_e$ can exceed 1 for some elements.
\end{itemize}
To ensure the formulation of $K_e$ is sensible at the design domain boundaries, we consider the space below the build plate to be an infinite half-plane of solid material, and all other volume outside the design volume to be void, both printed at $t=-\infty$, as shown in \autoref{fig:outside_domain}.

The hotspot severity $S_e$ for each element is then defined with $S_e = (1 - K_e)\frac{\rho_e}{\rho_e + \varepsilon_S}$, where the last term is used to ensure the severity of void elements is zero.

We wish to constrain the maximum severity across the entire part. To do this smoothly, we aggregate over the part with the KS function\footnote{We choose the KS function to preserve translation equivariance. Alternatively, $p$-norm aggregation could be used, which would preserve scale equivariance; however, here $S$ is already normalized to be of order 1 so translation equivariance is more important.}:
\[S = \delta_S + \frac{1}{\beta_S} \ln \sum_{j \in \Omega} e^{\beta_S S_j} \]
$\delta_S$ is a calibration term to ensure that $S$ stays approximately equal to the actual maximum without influencing gradients, as otherwise $S$ would overestimate the true average. $\delta_S$ is estimated using an exponential moving average.
Specifically, it follows the update rule
\[\delta_{S, n} = (1-\alpha) \delta_{S, n-1} + \alpha \left(\max_j S_j - \frac{1}{\beta_S} \ln \sum_{j \in \Omega} e^{\beta_S S_j}\right)\]
where $\delta_{S,i}$ is the value of $\delta_S$ on iteration $i$, and $\alpha$ is a constant hyperparameter controlling how quickly $\delta_S$ gets updated; we find $\alpha=0.1$ to work well. Alternatively, one can update $\delta_S$ periodically (for example, every $N$ iterations); however, updating too frequently can cause the optimizer to go in cycles from each jump while updating too rarely leads to an out-of-date aggregation.


\subsection{Optimization problem}
We optimize over density field $\rho$ and time-field design variables $\mu$ and $b$, solving the optimization problem:
\[
    \min_{\rho,\mu,b}\quad
    \log c(\rho) + w_u U(\rho, t)
    + w_\Phi \Phi(\rho, t)
    + w_S S\big(\operatorname{sg}[\rho], \mu, b\big)
    \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
    g_V = \frac{1}{N_e \bar V}\sum_e \rho_e - 1 \le 0.
\]
\begin{itemize}
    \item $c(\rho)$ is the compliance. We optimize $\log c(\rho)$ so that gradients are scale-invariant and weighting hyperparameters generalize better across different problems.
    \item $U(\rho, t)$ is the density-weighted coefficient of variation of the time gradient $g=\abs{\nabla t}$ and acts as a layer uniformity regularizer:
          \[
              U(\rho, t) = \frac{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon_U m^2}}{m},
              \qquad
              m = \frac{\sum_j \rho_j g_j}{\sum_j \rho_j},
              \qquad
              \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum_j \rho_j(g_j - m)^2}{\sum_j \rho_j}
          \]
    \item $\Phi(\rho, t)$ is a hotspot constraint penalty term, penalizing elements whose severity exceeds a critical severity $T_{cr}$:
          \[
              \Phi(\rho, t) = \frac{1}{N_e} \sum_{e} \rho_e \frac{1}{\beta_\Phi} \mathrm{softplus}\big(\beta_\Phi (S_e - T_{cr})\big), \qquad \mathrm{softplus}(x) = \ln \left(1 + e^x\right)
          \]
          where $\beta_\Phi$ controls the sharpness of softplus. This penalty formulation uses gradient pressure to remove density wherever an element has severity $S_e$ exceeding $T_{cr}$, while applying near zero pressure wherever $S_e < T_{cr}$, so it shapes the optimization dynamics with little effect on the optimum.
    \item $S(\operatorname{sg}[\rho], \mu, b)$ is the hotspot severity and its addition to the optimization objective pressures the time field to minimize the hotspot severity even when $S_e \leq T_{cr}$. $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes a stop-gradient (detaching $\rho$'s gradients); this includes the dependence of $t$ on $\rho$ through $\chi$. The purpose of this term is to optimize the time field first to reduce $S_e$, and only have the hotspot severity constraint influence $\rho$ if modifying the time field is insufficient to avoid overheating.
    \item $g_V$ is the volume fraction constraint, with $\bar V$ the maximum volume fraction.
    \item $w_u$, $w_\Phi$, and $w_S$ are weighting hyperparameters controlling the relative strengths of their associated terms.
\end{itemize}

$\Phi$ encodes the intuition that if a feature cannot be solidified without leading to overheating (e.g. thin members), it should be removed.
The penalty term $\Phi$ is used instead of a hard constraint on severity $S$ because the choice of $s$ makes the hotspot severity largely invariant to uniform changes in density, and so a hard constraint would provide negligible gradient pressure on a gray (i.e. partially solid) feature.

It is ineffective to encode this intuition as a hard constraint with a different formulation for $S$, because to do so would require a positive gradient of $S$ with respect to uniform changes in density, and therefore imply that a uniform reduction in density can be used to reduce $S$. However, this tends to cause thin members to become gray (at the point just before $S$ exceeds $T_{cr}$), rather than fully disappearing.

\subsection{Our time field formulation robustly avoids local minima and maxima in time}

To demostrate the advantage of the PDE-based formulation of the time field as opposed to directly optimizing the time field (i.e.\ having the time field values being the design variables, possibly with some low-pass filtering),

We consider the load case in \autoref{fig:tf_setup}: a square design domain, with fixed supports along the left edge, two equal point loads on the right corners at 45 degrees, and a build plate along the bottom.
To demonstrate the differences between different time formulations, we focus on fabrication sequence optimization, by first separately optimizing only the density field, then optimizing the time field to minimize hotspot severity with different formulations holding the density field fixed.
The resulting part geometry is shown in \autoref{fig:tf_pure_to}, which is a challenging case for optimizers because of the large flat member at the top of the domain.
We compare four different formulations for the time field:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \code{direct}: Directly optimizing the time field
    \item \code{sink_term}: Defining the time field as the solution of a heat equation with a sink term, as done in \cite{Wang2024Regularization}:
          \[\nabla \cdot (\rho \kappa \nabla \tau) - \alpha_T \tau = 0 \qquad t=1-\tau\]
          Here, $\tau$ represents a virtual temperature field which decreases monotonically from the print bed, $\kappa$ is the design variable, and $\alpha_T \tau$ is a drain term to ensure the solution is not constant. Scaling the diffusivity by the density $\rho$ prevents the time field from bridging across void regions to form local minima. This PDE has boundary conditions $\tau=1$ on the print bed and zero-flux elsewhere, and represents steady-state heat diffusion.
    \item \code{poisson}: The same heat equation, followed by a Poisson fit to the direction of $-\nabla \tau$, similar to \cite{Xu2025CurvedLayerSlicing}:
          \[\nabla \cdot (\rho \nabla t) = \nabla \cdot \left(\rho \mathbf{X}\right) \qquad \mathbf{X} = -\frac{\nabla \tau}{\abs{\nabla \tau}}\]
          with $t = 0$ on the print bed and zero flux of $\rho (\nabla t - \mathbf{X})$ elsewhere. This is the least-squares fit of $\nabla t$ to the unit vector field $\mathbf{X}$, so $\abs{\nabla t} \approx 1$ and layers have nearly uniform thickness. \cite{Xu2025CurvedLayerSlicing} use manually-specified non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions to generate a non-trivial time solution; we use a sink term to avoid needing to manually specify the boundary conditions.
    \item \code{optimized_bc}: Our PDE formulation of time, in \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace}
\end{itemize}
We use sink strength $\alpha_T = 1/A$ for \code{sink_term}, with $A$ the design domain area, which gave the lowest hotspot severity in a sweep over $0.3/A$ to $25/A$, and $\alpha_T = 25/A$ for \code{poisson}, whose hotspot severity is insensitive to $\alpha_T$ over that range.
All time fields are initialized as linear ramps from the bottom of the design volume to the top (\autoref{fig:tf_time_init}), to make the results more comparable to a full STTO run where the geometry is not known a-priori.

\subsection{Loose values of \texorpdfstring{$T_{cr}$}{Tcr} result in a design without a compliance penalty}
Given that this formulation treats overheating as a constraint, we expect that the part topology should change relative to an unconstrained design only if changing the time field is insufficient to satisfy the constraint.
Thus, for large enough values for $T_{cr}$, we should expect to see an identical design.
In practice, because the layer thickness uniformity regularizer includes density, optimization dynamics result in different converged design.
However, we find no compliance penalty in our experiments for large values of $T_{cr}$.
To evaluate this, we consider the cantilever beam from \autoref{sec:sim_vs_seq} and sweep $T_{cr}$.
A compliance penalty is only present for $T_{cr} \leq 0.5$.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
% (2 page). Discussion of the results and Conclusions connecting results to research or design question.

The presented approach represents a framework for addressing overheating; however, hyperparameters such as $r_K$ and the ideal shape of the radial and angular weights aren't optimized to represent a specific manufacturing process. While the exact choices for these hyperparameters likely do not impact the final design substantially, higher accuracy of predicted overheating risk can be achieved by calibrating these settings to match experimental data or higher-fidelity transient thermal simulations.

Theoretically, local minima (corresponding to unsupported material) on the final part design are still possible, because the mathematical guarantee againts internal local minima applies to the entire design volume, including void elements.
Therefore, elements on the boundary between solid and void could still have local minima when considering only the time field within the part.
In practice, this rarely happens, because the material would likely violate the hotspot severity constraint, since there would be no surrounding material printed earlier in time and $F(\Delta t) \approx 0$ (as defined in \autoref{sec:hotspot_formulation}).
This suggests that this approach, or a simple variant, could be used in other STTO formulations as a measure against local minima in time rather than for reducing overheating risk.

A limitation of the time field formulation is that saddle points are not ruled out in the time field.
Physically, saddle points are difficult or impossible to manufacture, since they have a zero radius of curvature as well as requiring material deposition from multiple sides.
This could be addressed by applying a maximum curvature constraint.

Some optimized designs include small holes which are smaller than the radius $r_K$ used to compute hotspot severity.
As noted by \cite{das2025overheating}, this can result in an underestimate of the hotspot severity, since material beyond the gap contributes to the estimate of heat evacuation capacity $K$ while not actually contributing.
\cite{das2025overheating} suggests using the robust formulation of topology optimization as introduced by \cite{Wang2011RobustProjection}.
Alternatively, a smaller value of $r_K$ could be used.
To re-incorporate longer-range effects, the heat evacuation capacity could be computed recursively, with elements weighted based on their heat evacuation capacity in the previous iteration.

Despite these limitations, the developed approach provides a promising framework to harness the flexibility of multi-axis AM to improve manufacturing outcomes.
The presented time field formulation provides a flexible yet resiliant parameterization, suitable for future development of space-time topology optimization techniques.
By simultaneously optimizing the fabrication sequence and topology, manufacturing constraints can influence the part design without being stricter than necessary, maximizing performance.

\section{Derivation of generality of time field formulation}
\label{sec:time_field_generality}

This appendix shows that \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} can represent any smooth time field that is zero on the build plate and has no critical points (points where $\nabla t = 0$).
The argument follows \cite{richter1981inverse}, who used it to recover a diffusion coefficient from a measured solution, and holds in any number of spatial dimensions.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:generality}
    Let $t$ be a twice continuously differentiable time field on a bounded domain $\Omega$, with $t = 0$ on the build plate $\partial\Omega_p$ and $\abs{\nabla t} \geq m > 0$ everywhere.
    Then there is a diffusivity $\chi$ with $e^{-Q} \leq \chi \leq e^{Q}$, where
    \[
        Q = \frac{\left(\max t - \min t\right) \max \abs{\Delta t}}{m^2},
    \]
    for which $t$ is the solution of \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} with $b = t$ on $\partial\Omega \backslash \partial\Omega_p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \emph{Step 1: an equation for $\chi$.}
    Writing $\chi = e^{w}$,
    \[
        \nabla \cdot \left(\chi \nabla t\right) = e^{w} \left(\Delta t + \nabla w \cdot \nabla t\right),
    \]
    so \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} holds if and only if
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla t \cdot \nabla w = -\Delta t.
        \label{eqn:transport}
    \end{equation}
    Here $t$ is known and $w$ is unknown.

    \emph{Step 2: solve along streamlines.}
    The left side of \autoref{eqn:transport} is the rate of change of $w$ along $\nabla t$, so it can be solved one streamline of $\nabla t$ at a time: the curves everywhere tangent to $\nabla t$, crossing the layers perpendicularly. As $\chi \nabla t$ points the same way, these are also the streamlines of the flux\footnote{In the language of partial differential equations, these streamlines are the \emph{characteristics} of \autoref{eqn:transport}.}.
    Measuring progress along a streamline by the print time $t$ itself, \autoref{eqn:transport} becomes an ordinary differential equation along each streamline:
    \[
        \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\Delta t}{\abs{\nabla t}^2}.
    \]
    Since $\nabla t$ never vanishes, $t$ increases steadily along every streamline, so each streamline starts and ends on the domain boundary and covers a time range of at most $\max t - \min t$.
    Setting $w = 0$ where each streamline starts and integrating gives $\abs{w} \leq Q$, hence the bounds on $\chi$\footnote{Neighbouring streamlines can start on different parts of the boundary, so $\chi$ can jump between them. This is harmless: the jump runs along streamlines of the flux, which no flux crosses, and \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} still holds in its usual integral (weak) form.}.

    \emph{Step 3: $t$ is the solution.}
    With $b = t$ on $\partial\Omega \backslash \partial\Omega_p$, and $t = 0$ on $\partial\Omega_p$ by assumption, $t$ satisfies both the equation and the boundary conditions of \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace}.
    Like steady heat conduction with a positive, bounded conductivity and fixed boundary temperatures, \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} has exactly one solution \cite[Section 8.2]{gilbarg2001elliptic}, so the optimizer's solve returns $t$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The build plate.}
The plate value $t = 0$ is fixed by the formulation rather than designed, so a representable time field must satisfy it; it does not restrict the choice of $\chi$.
$\chi$ is also not unique: other starting values of $w$ in Step 2, or scaling $\chi$ by a constant, represent the same $t$.

\paragraph{Critical points.}
Requiring $\nabla t \neq 0$ everywhere is stronger than having no interior local minima or maxima: it also excludes saddle points, which is where two print fronts merge.
It cannot simply be dropped: at a point where $\nabla t = 0$, \autoref{eqn:time_field_laplace} with a smooth $\chi$ reduces to $\chi \Delta t = 0$, so only saddles with $\Delta t = 0$ there (e.g.\ $t = x_1^2 - x_2^2$ with $\chi = 1$) are representable.
Saddles are treated in \cite{alessandrini1986identification}, but only in two dimensions, so we do not rely on it here.

\paragraph{Range of $\chi$.}
In solid material ($\rho = 1$), the implementation restricts $\chi = C^{\mu - 0.5}$ with $\mu \in [0, 1]$ to a window spanning a factor $C$.
As scaling $\chi$ by a constant does not change $t$, \autoref{prop:generality} shows that a time field over a fully solid domain is representable within this window whenever $e^{2Q} \leq C$; this is a sufficient, generally conservative, condition.
